    DFS in three orders (pre, in, post) and BFS level by level. Nearly every tree question is one of these with a small twist, and the recursive solution is usually short — the skill is deciding what each call should return up to its parent.

  2. Binary search tree

    A tree with an ordering invariant, which makes an in-order traversal produce sorted output — the fact that most BST problems hinge on. Know why a degenerate BST becomes a linked list, and what balancing (AVL, red-black) exists to prevent that.

  4. Graph traversal — BFS & DFS

    Once you see a grid, a set of dependencies or a network as a graph, traversal solves it. BFS gives shortest path in an unweighted graph and is the right choice for "minimum steps"; DFS suits connectivity and exhaustive exploration. Always track visited.

  5. Topological sort

    Ordering a directed acyclic graph so every dependency comes before its dependent — build systems, course prerequisites, task scheduling. Kahn's algorithm (BFS on in-degrees) also detects cycles for free, which is usually half the question.

  6. Shortest path & weighted graphs

    When edges have costs, BFS stops working. Dijkstra with a heap for non-negative weights, Bellman-Ford when weights can be negative or hops are capped, and the observation that many "minimise the maximum" grid problems are Dijkstra in disguise.

    Near-constant-time "are these two connected?" and "merge these two groups", with path compression and union by rank. The right tool whenever connectivity changes over time, and the basis of Kruskal's minimum spanning tree.
